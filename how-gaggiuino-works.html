How Gaggiuino Works

What the conversion adds, what each new part does, and how they work together: the PCB that runs everything, the sensors it reads, the way it holds the temperature and drives the pump, and the optional scales, tank sensor and switch lights.

Who does what now

before and after

On a stock Silvia the switches and thermostats do the controlling themselves: each one sits in a mains wire and either completes a load's circuit or breaks it. Gaggiuino moves every decision into one place.

The brew, hot water and steam switches stop carrying mains and become signals. A thermocouple and a pressure sensor report what's happening. The PCB reads all of it many times a second and decides what the element, pump and 3-way valve should do, switching the pump and valve itself and the element through a solid-state relay. The screen shows it and takes your settings.

What stays: the power switch still breaks mains, the 165 °C safety thermostat still sits in the element's supply, a pump thermal fuse stays if yours has one, and earth is bonded exactly as before. None of those depend on software.

Holding the temperature

thermocouple, PCB, SSR

The thermocouple in the boiler top reports the temperature continuously, the PCB decides when the element should be on, and the SSR switches it. Instead of the stock thermostat's long on and long off, the element gets short pulses as the boiler nears the target, so the temperature settles there instead of swinging around it.

Gen 3's code isn't public, but it uses the same settings as the open Gen 2 firmware. In the Gen 2 code it works in steps. More than 10 °C below the target the element is simply on. From 10 to 5 °C below it's pulsed for a fixed share of the time, and in the last 5 °C for a smaller share, with the pulses getting shorter as the boiler closes in. At the target it's off. During a shot it runs nearly flat out to meet the cold water coming in. That code isn't a PID loop: each step is a fixed rule. Whether Gen 3 changed the details isn't published.

The settings that shape it

  • HPWR: the base time, in milliseconds, that the two dividers scale; the Gen 3 manual calls it the relay's maximum pulse width. It sets how long the on and off pulses last, and they shrink as the boiler closes in.
  • Main Divider: sets the share of time the element is on from 10 to 5 °C below the target, one part in Main Divider + 1. At 5 that's one part in six.
  • Brew Divider: the same for the last 5 °C, one part in Brew Divider² + 1. At 3 that's one part in ten.
  • Brew Delta: during a shot, lets the boiler run above the target in step with the flow, so the cold water coming in meets heat straight away. The docs compare it to a thermoblock.
  • Offset: the gap between the boiler reading and the water at the group. The firmware adds it to the profile's temperature to get the boiler target.

Gaggiuino's defaults are tuned for the Gaggia Classic. Silvia builders on Discord start from HPWR 100, Main Divider 5, Brew Divider 3, Brew Delta off and Offset 5; they're in the starting settings on the home page.

Driving the pump

pressure and flow profiling

A vibratory pump can't be slowed down like a motor: every stroke is a fixed squirt. So the PCB controls how many strokes happen. A zero-cross detector tells the STM32 each time the mains wave passes zero, and for every cycle it decides whether to fire the triac that feeds the pump. Asking for 50% lets every other cycle through, 25% one in four, spread as evenly as possible. This is pulse skip modulation, PSM.

Pressure profiling closes the loop on the pressure sensor: the PCB reads it many times a second and raises or lowers the share of cycles to follow the profile's curve. Flow profiling works the other way round. Gaggiuino carries a model of the Ulka pump that says how much water one stroke moves at a given pressure, so it can turn a target flow into strokes per second, and it estimates the flow during every shot the same way. Pump Zero is that model's flow per stroke at 0 bar; the [UT] PZ Cal profile calibrates it against your scale, since no two pumps are quite alike.

The OPV still sits above all of this. Whatever a profile asks for, the pressure can't pass the OPV's setting, which is why Gaggiuino wants it at least 1 bar above your highest target.

The new parts

what each one does

The PCB

mounted beside the 3-way valve or the pump

The control board. Its STM32 microcontroller, an STM32U585 on PCB v4, runs the machine: it reads every sensor and switch, runs the shot profile, and switches the loads. Mains comes in on J10 (neutral) and J11 (line) and feeds a sealed 5 V power supply module on the board. The pump on J12 and the 3-way valve on J13 are switched at mains voltage by solid-state parts on the board, so nothing clicks. The STM32, the sensor chips and the low-voltage headers sit on the other side of an isolation gap, and nothing on the board links the two halves with a wire.

What's on it

  • A MAX31855 chip reads the thermocouple, and an ADS1015 converter reads the pressure sensor's voltage.
  • The pump is switched by a triac, fired through an optocoupler. A zero-cross detector, also behind an optocoupler, tells the STM32 each time the mains wave passes zero, and the STM32 decides cycle by cycle whether to fire it.
  • The 3-way valve is switched by a small optically isolated solid-state relay; a red LED beside it lights whenever the valve is powered.
  • J9 gives the boiler's SSR 5 V on R1 when the element should be on.

New firmware goes on over Wi-Fi from the web page: the screen's, and on PCB v4 the board's own. A board that won't take an update is flashed by wire through its SWD pins (J27) with an ST-Link or WeAct debugger. The USB-C port only powers the board on the bench.

Solid-state relay (SSR)

screwed to the left wall

The switch for the element. It's an SSR-40DA: a relay controlled by 3 to 32 V DC that switches AC, with no moving contacts. J9 R1 feeds its + input and J9 GND its −. Its two load screws sit in the element's supply, ahead of the safety thermostat.

How it works

The input current lights an LED inside the relay. On the other side of an insulating gap, a light-sensitive switch sees it and fires a triac, a semiconductor switch for AC, which carries the element current. The PCB and mains only meet through light, which is what keeps the low-voltage side isolated. A zero-cross SSR only turns on where the mains wave crosses zero, and a triac lets go at the next zero, so the element switches cleanly in whole half-cycles.

While it conducts it drops up to 1.6 V, which at a Silvia's element current turns several watts into heat, so it needs to sit flat on metal. Fork spades on all four screws, never a bare wire under a screw, and buy from a known seller: counterfeit SSR-40DAs with undersized triacs are common.

Thermocouple

boiler top, to J4

The temperature sensor. Silvia builders on Discord, and GaggiMate's Silvia guide, use an ungrounded K-type with an M3 thread, screwed into one of the holes the brew or steam thermostat's clamp used, with thermal paste and only finger tight. Gaggiuino's own parts list has an M4 probe, for Gaggias. If your boiler was made before May 2000, check how one mounts before you buy.

How it works

Two different alloys, chromel and alumel, are joined at the tip. When the tip is hotter than the far ends, a tiny voltage appears between them, about 41 microvolts per degree. The MAX31855 chip on the PCB measures it, adds the temperature at its own end (the cold junction), and hands the STM32 a reading. "Ungrounded" means the junction is insulated inside the probe's sheath, which matters because the boiler is earthed; see why the low-voltage side floats.

Polarity matters. Swapped wires make the reading fall as the boiler heats, so the controller may keep heating. The public Gen 2 firmware cuts the heater once the reading drops to 0 °C, Gen 3's behavior isn't published, and the 165 °C safety thermostat is the last line. That's why the runbook has you watch the temperature rise at first power-on.

Pressure sensor

on a tee in the water line, to J6

A 0 to 1.2 MPa (0 to 12 bar) pressure transducer with a 1/4" thread, powered from 5 V. It's what lets Gaggiuino profile pressure: the PCB reads it continuously and adjusts the pump to follow the curve.

How it works

Water pressure bends a thin metal diaphragm. Strain gauges on the diaphragm change resistance as it flexes, and electronics in the sensor body turn that into a voltage on the signal pin: 0.5 V at 0 bar, rising evenly to 4.5 V at 12 bar. The ADS1015 on the PCB converts that voltage to a number.

Most Silvia builds on Discord put it on a 1/8" tee at the OPV, through an elbow that keeps it clear of the steam pipes; some use the 3-way valve's bottom port, and both work. The tapered fittings take Loctite 55. The sensor seals on an O-ring, not its thread: press the flat 11 × 6 mm O-ring flush into the elbow's 1/4" socket first, because the sensor's own base O-ring often can't reach the seat, then fit it hand tight plus at most a quarter turn. [UT] Tube Fill pushes the air out of its line.

Screen

on top, to J1

The 4.3" touch screen is a small computer of its own, built on an ESP32-S3 Wi-Fi chip. It draws the interface, stores your profiles and settings, runs the Gaggiuino Wi-Fi network and the web page at gaggiuino.local, and installs updates for itself and the board. It talks to the PCB over a serial link on J1, which also powers it.

The cable is wired by pin, not by color: blue and yellow swap between cable revisions. With a funnel mount on a Silvia it needs a longer cable than a Gaggia's front mount.

The harness

32 runs, each labeled at both ends

The Silvia has no plug-in Gaggiuino harness, so the stock wiring comes out and a new one goes in, built from realNiche's diagram: 16 mains runs (including two earth runs), 11 low-voltage runs and 5 optional ones. Mains runs are 18 AWG silicone wire rated to 200 °C, low-voltage runs 22 AWG. Bare spades with self-fusing silicone tape go on the hot ends at the boiler and valve, two-wire spades wherever two runs share a terminal, and fork spades on the SSR.

Mains is routed high and low voltage low, kept apart, with nothing but silicone wire touching the boiler or steam wand. Wiring reference, cut list, labels.

Switches on low voltage

J7 and J5

The brew, hot water and steam rockers stay on the front panel but only report now. Each one, when on, connects a PCB input pin to the low-voltage ground: steam to J7 S, brew to J7 B, hot water to J5 B15, with a ground chain across all three to J7 GND.

How it works

A pull-up resistor holds each input at 3.3 V: 10 kΩ on the board for brew and steam, and the STM32's own built-in one for hot water on J5. So an open switch reads high, which the firmware treats as off. Closing the switch ties the pin to ground and it reads low: on. That's why the power-on checks want under 1 Ω with a switch on and OL with it off.

None of the stock mains wiring can stay on these switches. Mains on an input pin destroys it, and wiring the pump around the PCB's pump output can short the triac that drives it.

The public Gen 2 firmware reads a separate hot water switch on B15. The Gen 3 manual only describes the Gaggia's brew-plus-steam combination, so whether Gen 3 reads the Silvia's own hot water switch is still being confirmed on Discord.

Optional mods

add any time

Hardware scales

under the drip tray, to J8

Two 1 kg load cells on a printed mount under the drip tray area weigh the cup as the shot runs, so Gaggiuino can stop at a target weight and plot it live. Without them it still predicts the weight from the pump's flow; a Bluetooth scale is the other option.

How it works

A load cell is a metal bar with strain gauges glued to it, wired as a bridge. The weight of the cup bends the bar by a tiny amount and the bridge puts out a few millivolts at most. Each cell has its own HX711 channel, which amplifies and measures that very finely; the two share the clock line on J8 and each has its own data line, D1 and D2. The PCB adds the two, subtracts the tare and converts to grams after you calibrate with a known weight.

Gaggiuino's parts list has 750 g cells. On a Silvia the mount of choice is Joscha's "beefy" remix, Printables 1277828, built for 13 × 13 × 80 mm 1 kg cells.

Tank level sensor

above the tank, to J2

The ToFnLED board's distance sensor, mounted above the water, tells the screen how full the tank is, so you see it running low before the pump runs dry. It plugs into the Qwiic socket, J2, on the back of the PCB. The board also has an RGB LED to light the tank; the Silvia's tank is covered, so builders use it for the level reading.

How it works

A time-of-flight sensor fires a very short pulse of infrared light and times how long the reflection takes to come back from the water surface. Light covers a millimeter in about 3 trillionths of a second, so the chip, a VL53L0X in the Gen 2 code, has to time it extremely finely; it reports a distance, and the firmware turns the distance into a percentage using the ranges you set. The Silvia's tank is deeper than a Gaggia's, about 200 mm, so those ranges need changing in the ToFnLED settings.

Switch lights

inside the three rockers

Once the brew, hot water and steam switches are on low voltage, their mains lamps go dark. The LED mod replaces each lamp with a small orange LED and resistor powered from the 5 V screen line, wired across the switch's spare pole so the light follows the switch. The power switch keeps its stock lamp.

Why the low-voltage side floats

The machine's metal, the frame, boiler and valve bodies, is bonded to earth for safety. The PCB's 5 V side is deliberately left floating: its ground is not connected to earth anywhere. Everything that crosses between them does so without a wire: the board's sealed power supply module, three optocouplers on the board (for the zero-cross detector, the pump and the 3-way valve) and the SSR's light link. The board's own mains section is live whenever the machine is plugged in.

The reason is the thermocouple. Its tip is screwed into the earthed boiler, and the MAX31855 that reads it must never have its minus input tied to its ground; if it does, it reports a short to ground or reads wrong. An ungrounded thermocouple, with the junction insulated inside the probe, keeps the tip electrically off the boiler, and a floating 5 V side keeps the two from meeting some other way.

So never bond the 5 V ground to earth, and watch for accidental paths: nothing on the low-voltage side should touch the chassis, including a metal screw through one of the board's mounting holes, and a desktop computer on the USB port, which is usually earthed, can make the connection for you. The power-on checks include an open-circuit test between earth and J7 GND.
